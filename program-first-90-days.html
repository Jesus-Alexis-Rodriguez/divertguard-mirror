First 90 Days of a Drug Diversion Program

A sequenced plan for a hospital starting a program: what to do in each two-week block, who owns it, what it produces, and what goes wrong if it is skipped.

The plan has 6 blocks and 18 steps over 12 weeks. Each step names one action, the role that owns it, the artifact it leaves behind, and the failure it prevents. Where a step needs a time window or a threshold, you set it from your own policy and your own data. This page gives no benchmark, because a number from another hospital will not carry over to your cabinets, counting rules and drug mix.

Jump to: Weeks 1-2 · Weeks 3-4 · Weeks 5-6 · Weeks 7-8 · Weeks 9-10 · Weeks 11-12. The working files are on the toolkit page.

How to use it. Owners are roles. Substitute the people in your organization, and merge roles where one person holds both. The artifacts are your evidence that the step happened, and the “if skipped” column is the reason to do it when a week is tight. This is a planning guide, not legal or regulatory advice; check your obligations with your compliance officer and counsel.

After day 90

At the end of week 12 you should be able to show a named owner, a scoped inventory, a reconciled user list, one indicator with a calibrated threshold, written documentation standards with trained staff, one full audit cycle and a first scorecard. Whatever is not finished goes onto the next quarter’s plan with an owner and a date. From here the monthly rhythm on the KPIs page takes over, and each new case adds a row to the case index.

Questions

Why does the plan start with scope instead of software?

Because every later step depends on knowing where controlled substances are stored, dispensed, administered and wasted, and which system records each step. A tool bought or switched on before that inventory is built on a guess about what it covers.

Can a smaller pharmacy or hospital shorten the plan?

Yes. The order matters more than the pace: scope, then access and configuration, then data, then documentation and training, then an audit cycle, then reporting. A small site may finish a phase in days, and a large system may need to run phases in parallel by unit. Keep each artifact even when the phase is short.

Which indicator should we start with?

One that your data can support today and that someone will act on. Override review is a common first choice because it uses a cabinet report the pharmacy already has, but the right first indicator depends on your cabinet fields and your risk assessment. Run it silently against history before you set a line.

What if a step shows a problem we cannot fix in 90 days?

Record it as a program risk with an owner and a review date, and carry on. The plan produces evidence of what is true, not a guarantee that everything is fixed. A known and tracked gap is better than an unknown one.
