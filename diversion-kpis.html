Drug Diversion KPIs, Detection Thresholds and Dashboards

What a diversion program measures, the thresholds that make a number worth a second look, how a signal climbs from a rule to a case, and the three dashboards that keep a pharmacy team working from the same facts.

In short. A diversion program measures three things: how unusual one person’s controlled-substance activity is against peers doing the same work (detection), how fast and how completely each signal is worked to a recorded outcome (response), and whether the controls that prevent diversion are actually being followed (compliance). Detection indicators are computed per person, unit and drug and compared with a local baseline; program KPIs are computed per period and reviewed by the team. Neither is proof of anything: every number below is a prompt to look, not a conclusion.

Jump to the detection-indicator table or the program-KPI table. For the qualitative warning signs (behavior, documentation, inventory), see the 15 red flags; this page is the measured companion to that list.

How diversion shows up in the data

Each scheme below leaves a pattern in cabinet, order, count or prescribing data. The pattern is what a rule can look for; the explanation is what a reviewer has to establish.

Excessive dispensing or withdrawal frequency

One clinician removes controlled substances far more often than peers assigned to similar patients. The pattern is a high removal count per shift worked, often concentrated in one drug, that is out of proportion to the unit’s acuity.

High override rate or waste not witnessed

Overrides skip pharmacist order verification, and unwitnessed waste skips the second set of eyes. A person who uses either far more than peers is working around the two controls designed to stop a solo diversion.

Reverse distribution and return-to-stock manipulation

Product is removed, then returned or sent back in a way that hides a shortage: a tampered or substituted item returned to stock, or returns that do not reconcile with what was removed. The signal is a high return rate for one user, or returns whose count or seal does not match.

Cancelled-then-reordered orders

An order is cancelled after a removal, or cancelled and re-entered so that a second removal can be made against it. The pattern is a high cancel rate tied to one user’s removals, or removals against orders that no longer exist.

Administration at shift edges or on days off

Removals cluster in the first and last minutes of a shift, when handoff blurs accountability, or occur when the person is not scheduled. The data shows removals timed against the schedule or time clock instead of the patient’s orders.

Disproportionate prescribing by provider

One prescriber’s controlled-substance prescribing sits far from peers in the same specialty after adjusting for volume. Diverted prescriptions, forged scripts and pill-mill patterns appear as outliers in a provider-level comparison.

Cabinet access outside assigned unit hours

A user pulls from a cabinet on a unit they are not assigned to that shift, or outside their scheduled hours, usually to reach stock with less scrutiny. The signal is access events with no matching assignment or patient.

Quantity discrepancies at cycle count

Counts do not match the perpetual inventory, repeatedly, on the same unit or drug. A single discrepancy is usually an error; a run of them that share a last user or a time window is a pattern.

Repeated “patient refused” with no witness

A dose is removed, documented as refused or not given, and returned or wasted without a witness. The pattern is a refusal rate well above peers, especially where the refusal never reaches the patient’s record the same way twice.

Diversion of waste (partial vials)

The unused portion of a vial or syringe is taken instead of discarded, or waste is documented in full while the witness signs without seeing it. Look for high wasted-dose rates, waste documented seconds after removal, or the same witness appearing repeatedly with one user.

Real cases that turned on these methods are in the diversion case library and the searchable case database; the registry totals are on the open data page.

Detection indicators and thresholds

The table gives twelve indicators: how each is computed, a starting threshold, what it usually means, and the ordinary reason it fires for someone who is doing nothing wrong. Most are expressed as a ratio to a peer baseline (the median for the same unit, role and shift type), because a raw count means little without knowing how much work the person did and where.

Read this before using any threshold. Every threshold is a starting point that must be tuned against your own baseline: your cabinets, your units, your drug mix and your EHR feed all change what “normal” is. Run each rule silently for a few weeks, look at who it flags, and adjust until reviewers can work the volume. And a flag is a hypothesis, not a finding.

How signals bubble up

A rule firing is the start of the process, not the end. The ladder below keeps volume manageable and makes sure every alert ends with a recorded outcome.

  1. An automated rule fires. A scheduled query or the monitoring tool evaluates an indicator and produces an alert for a person, drug and date range.
  2. It is queued with context. The alert arrives with the peer comparison (the user’s value against the unit median), the number of patients involved, the shift and unit, and any other indicator already flagged for the same person. An alert with no context cannot be triaged quickly.
  3. A clinical or pharmacy reviewer triages it within a stated window. As an illustrative starting point, one business day for a standard alert and the same shift where patients may be at risk. The reviewer checks assignment, acuity and downtime and either closes it with a reason or escalates.
  4. An investigation is opened with an evidence package. The package holds the transaction detail, the peer comparison, chart review against orders, administration and pain scores, and the timeline. From here the investigation playbook applies.
  5. The outcome is recorded and reported. Substantiated, unsubstantiated or process failure, with the action taken, and any required regulatory or licensing report filed on time (see the DEA 106 filing guide). The outcome feeds the scorecard.

What makes an alert worth escalating

  • Recurrence. The same person triggers the same rule again in a rolling window after an earlier alert closed without action.
  • Co-occurring signals. Two or more independent indicators point at the same person or drug at the same time.
  • Patient-safety exposure. Missing or compromised medication for patients, such as a returned vial that may have been tampered with, which should move straight to the same-shift path.

What closes it as a false positive

  • A verified, documented explanation checked against a source (the assignment sheet, the patient’s acuity, a downtime log), not the user’s word alone.
  • The pattern does not recur after the next review period.
  • The reviewer records the reason, and a repeated cause (for example one unit’s routine overrides) becomes a rule exclusion or a tuned threshold. Closed alerts are kept, never deleted, because a later alert may need them.

Program KPIs

Detection indicators describe people and units. Program KPIs describe the program: whether it looks at enough, closes what it opens, and is followed. Together they are the scorecard that keeps pharmacy, nursing and compliance aligned. The targets are illustrative and local; they are not hospital benchmarks, and none of them is drawn from a published study.

The three dashboards that do the work

Each dashboard has one audience, one cadence and one decision. Putting everything on one screen is the usual way a dashboard stops being used. The analytics dashboard guide has sample SQL and layout for building the tiles, and the Power BI reference covers the data model.

A. Daily investigation queue

Audience: the diversion investigator. Cadence: every business day.

  1. Alerts by age. Open alerts in 0–1, 2–3 and over 3 days. Bad: anything past the triage window.
  2. Top clinicians by peer ratio. Ranked list of users with their ratio to the unit median. Bad: a ratio near 2x or more with real volume behind it.
  3. Co-occurring signals. Users flagged on two or more indicators. Bad: any user on the list at all.
  4. Patient exposure. Patients tied to the flagged removals and whether pain scores and administration support them. Bad: removals with no matching administration.
  5. Timeline for a selected user. Removals plotted against schedule and orders. Bad: removals outside shift hours or with no order.
  6. Open cases and next action due. Bad: an overdue next step.

Decision it drives: which alerts to open as investigations today, and who is worked next.

B. Weekly pharmacy-operations

Audience: the pharmacy manager. Cadence: weekly.

  1. Discrepancy rate per 1,000 doses by unit. Twelve-week trend per unit. Bad: a unit climbing against its own baseline.
  2. Unresolved discrepancies past window. Count and age. Bad: any older than the policy allows.
  3. Override rate by unit. With the top documented reasons. Bad: a rising rate with a vague or missing reason.
  4. Unwitnessed waste. Count by unit and drug. Bad: a unit with repeat entries.
  5. Returns and reverse-distribution queue. Items waiting and those that failed reconciliation. Bad: any failed reconciliation.
  6. Counts and audits completed. Scheduled against done. Bad: a missed cycle count.

Decision it drives: which unit or process gets a fix, extra counts or staff time this week.

C. Monthly executive scorecard

Audience: the compliance committee. Cadence: monthly.

  1. KPI scorecard. The eight program KPIs against local targets, with a trend arrow. Bad: more than one moving the wrong way.
  2. Median days, signal to closure. With the age of open cases. Bad: a rising median or cases that never close.
  3. Investigations by outcome. Opened and closed, split into substantiated, unsubstantiated and process failure, counts only. Bad: many opened, few closed.
  4. Repeat-signal escalation. People with repeat alerts and how many were reviewed. Bad: any not reviewed.
  5. Training completion by role. Bad: one role well below the rest.
  6. Reporting status. Required reports due, filed and filed on time. Bad: any filed late.

Decision it drives: whether to fund, staff or change policy, and whether the program closes what it opens. Counts only; no names.

Calibrating a threshold for your own hospital

The starting thresholds in the table are placeholders. Turning one into a line your reviewers can live with takes six steps, done once per indicator and per peer group, then repeated on a schedule.

  1. Pick one indicator. Calibrate one at a time, beginning with the one that has the cleanest data (override rate is usually easiest). A threshold tuned on a messy feed tunes the mess.
  2. Write the numerator and denominator in full sentences. For example: numerator = override removals of controlled substances by the user in the window; denominator = all controlled-substance removals by the same user in the same window. Decide in writing what counts: which drug schedules, whether returns net out, whether downtime transactions are in or out, and the window length (30 days for a fast signal, 90 for a stable one). If two analysts could count it differently, it is not defined yet.
  3. Choose the comparison group. Three options, each with a cost:
    • Peer role (for example all RNs on the same unit and shift type). Best default: same patients, same cabinet, same work. It needs enough peers; a unit with four nurses cannot give a distribution.
    • Peer unit (this unit against similar units). Use for unit-level indicators such as discrepancy rate, and where individual peer groups are too small. Units differ in acuity, so match by type (ICU to ICU), not hospital-wide.
    • Own 12-month history. Catches a change in one person or unit, and works when there are no peers (a sole anesthesia tech). It cannot catch someone who has always been high, and it needs a year of clean data.
    Many programs run peer role as the screen and own history as a second view.
  4. Set the line. Three workable methods:
    • Percentile of the peer distribution (for example the 95th). Simple to explain, and it controls alert volume directly: about 5% of peers will sit above it by construction. It says nothing about whether the gap matters.
    • Peer z-score (user rate minus peer mean, divided by peer standard deviation; 2 for watch, 3 for review is a common convention). Handles groups with different spread, but it assumes a roughly bell-shaped distribution and is distorted by one extreme peer.
    • A rate that is both statistically unusual and operationally meaningful. Require the statistical test and a minimum excess in plain counts, for example at least five more overrides than the peer rate predicts. This is the one that protects reviewer time, because it stops a statistically unusual but trivial difference from becoming an alert.
  5. Apply a minimum denominator before anything can fire. See the sample-size trap below. Set it per indicator, write it next to the threshold, and show the denominator in every alert.
  6. Run silently, then go live. Run the rule for a few weeks without sending alerts, read who it would have flagged, and adjust until the volume is something your analyst can work (see the FAQ on alert volume).

The sample-size trap

A rate built on a handful of events swings wildly. A nurse who removed three controlled doses in the window and overrode two of them has a 67% override rate, which would top any peer list, yet two overrides in three removals is not much evidence about anything. Peers with 300 removals are being compared on a different footing from someone with 3. Without a minimum, the top of every ranked list fills with part-time, float and new staff, reviewers learn that the list is noise, and real signals get the same shrug.

Rule of thumb: pick a minimum denominator large enough that the expected number of events at the peer rate is at least a handful (here, 30 removals gives about four expected overrides), show the interval around the rate, and carry people under the minimum forward into the next window rather than dropping them, so low-volume activity adds up and is eventually seen.

Worked calculation (illustration only: invented numbers, not a benchmark)

Indicator: override rate. Window: trailing 90 days. Group: 22 RNs on one ICU, night shift. Numerator: override removals by the nurse. Denominator: all controlled-substance removals by the same nurse.

  1. Peer distribution. The 22 peer rates run from 6% to 23%. Mean 13.1%, median 12.5%, standard deviation 4.5 percentage points.
  2. Percentile line. The 95th percentile by nearest rank is the 21st of 22 sorted values: 21%.
  3. z-score line. z of 2 is 13.1% + 2 × 4.5 = 22.2%.
  4. Operational line. At the peer rate, a nurse with 60 removals is expected to have 60 × 0.131 = 7.9 overrides. Require at least 5 more than expected. At 60 removals that is 13 overrides (22%), which matches the z line. At 30 removals the expected count is 3.9, so 9 overrides are needed (30%): the line rises on its own as the denominator shrinks.
  5. The rule you write down: alert when removals in 90 days ≥ 30, rate ≥ 22%, and overrides exceed the peer-rate expectation by ≥ 5.
  6. Test it on two nurses. Nurse A: 18 overrides in 60 removals, 30%; z = (30 − 13.1) ÷ 4.5 = 3.7; excess = 18 − 7.9 = 10.1; fires. Nurse B: 2 overrides in 3 removals, 67%; z would be 12, but 3 < 30, so the rule cannot fire. The chance that a nurse with a true 13% rate shows 2 or more overrides in 3 removals is about 5%, so without the minimum roughly one in twenty low-volume nurses would be flagged by chance alone.
  7. Reading the interval. A 95% Wilson interval for Nurse A (18 of 60) runs from about 20% to 43%, clear of the 13% peer rate. For 8 of 60 (13%) it runs from about 7% to 24%, which says nothing unusual. The interval for Nurse B (2 of 3) is roughly 21% to 94%: far too wide to act on, which is the same point the minimum makes.

Still a hypothesis: Nurse A fires, and the next step is the triage in the worked month below, not a conclusion.

Review every quarter. Once a quarter, for each live threshold, check: how many alerts fired; how many closed as explained, and for what repeating reasons; whether any case was found by another route (a tip, a count) that the rule should have caught; and whether the peer distribution has shifted (new cabinet, new unit, staffing mix). Change a threshold only with a short written reason and the date, so the next quarter can see what moved and why. Alert volume falling after a change is not proof the change was right; check the missed-case question first.

What counts as closed, and who signs

A case is closed only when all of these are recorded, and the case system should refuse to close it otherwise:

  • The evidence package is filed (transaction detail, peer comparison, chart review, timeline, any interview notes).
  • An outcome category is chosen: substantiated; explained (a verified, documented reason, recorded as unsubstantiated or as a process failure depending on whether the cause was a person or a process); or referred to HR, a licensing board or another body under your policy.
  • The action taken has an owner and a date, including any rule change, exclusion or process fix.
  • Any required report is filed, or the decision that none is required is written down with the reasoning (see the DEA 106 filing guide).
  • The reviewed alerts are tagged with the outcome so they feed the next calibration.

Suggested sign-off, adapted to your structure: the analyst prepares the closure; the program lead approves closure of alerts and of explained investigations; the compliance officer signs off any investigation that is substantiated or referred; pharmacy leadership is informed through the monthly scorecard as a count. Closure is a statement that the work is done and documented, not that nothing was wrong.

A flag stays a hypothesis until the records say otherwise.

Limits and governance

  • Aggregates in board materials. Board and leadership materials carry counts and rates only. Named-person data stays in the case system.
  • Minimum cell size. Set a minimum count below which a subgroup (a unit, a role, a shift) is not reported on its own, and combine or suppress it instead. A small number often identifies one person. The minimum is a local decision; five is a common starting convention.
  • Relation to DEA recordkeeping. KPI data is derived from the required controlled-substance records and does not replace them. A scorecard is a management report, not a compliance record, and it does not show that the required records exist. Which records are required and how long they are kept is set out on the recordkeeping checklist (federal rules are a floor; a stricter state rule controls).
  • Retention. Keep closed alerts and their outcomes for as long as your policy and counsel say, and at least as long as the underlying records must be kept (two years under federal rules, per the recordkeeping checklist; your state may require longer). Retire a threshold or exclusion by dating it, not by deleting its history.
  • Who may see named-person data. Only the people working the case: the analyst or investigator, the program lead, the compliance officer, and HR and legal counsel where the case reaches them. Pharmacy leadership and the board see counts. Log who opened a case file.
  • Not legal advice. This page is operational guidance. Check anything that touches reporting, employment or privacy with HR and counsel.

What we would not claim

  • Thresholds vary. What counts as unusual depends on the institution, the EHR and the cabinet vendor, which differ in what they record and how. The thresholds here are starting points, not standards.
  • Published statistics are unreliable. Figures on how much diversion occurs describe the diversion that was detected, and most diversion is never detected, so they undercount it and say more about detection than occurrence. We give no prevalence figure for that reason.
  • This is operational guidance, not legal advice. Reporting duties, employment actions and privacy rules differ by state and facility. Consult HR and legal counsel before acting on a finding.

Frequently asked questions

What dispensing frequency should trigger review?

There is no universal number. A reasonable starting point is a clinician whose controlled-substance removals per shift worked are about twice the median of peers on the same unit, role and shift type, over a rolling 30 days, with a minimum volume so that two removals against one cannot fire the rule. Tune the ratio against your own baseline, and treat the flag as a hypothesis to check against patient assignment and acuity, not as a finding.

How many KPIs should a diversion program track?

Enough to cover detection, response and compliance, and few enough that each has an owner and a decision attached. This page proposes eight program KPIs and three dashboards of five or six tiles each. If a number does not change what someone does on Monday, drop it from the scorecard and keep it in the detail data.

What is a normal discrepancy rate?

This page does not give a normal rate because no sourced benchmark is available to us, and a rate from another hospital would not transfer across different cabinets, counting rules and drug mixes. Measure discrepancies per 1,000 doses for each unit, build a trailing 90-day baseline for that unit, and review the unit when it runs at roughly twice its own median or when one person is the last user in a disproportionate share of its discrepancies.

Who should own the diversion dashboard?

One named program lead should own the data definitions across all three dashboards so a number means the same thing everywhere. The audience differs by dashboard: the diversion investigator works the daily queue, the pharmacy manager reviews the weekly operations view, and the compliance committee reviews the monthly scorecard, which should carry counts and rates, never names.

Is a flagged alert evidence of diversion?

No. An alert is a hypothesis generated by a rule. It becomes worth escalating when it recurs, when it co-occurs with other signals, or when patients are exposed to risk, and it closes as a false positive when a documented, verified explanation accounts for the pattern. Only an investigation with an evidence package produces a finding.

How quickly should an alert be reviewed?

The escalation ladder on this page uses one business day for a standard alert and the same shift where a patient-safety exposure is suspected, as illustrative starting windows. Whatever window you choose should be written into policy and measured, which is what the time-from-event-to-detection indicator and the median-days-to-closure KPI are for.

How many alerts a month is normal?

There is no normal number, and this page gives none because no sourced figure exists that would transfer between hospitals. Work backwards from capacity instead: decide how many alerts one analyst can triage properly in a week, run the rule silently for a few weeks, and set the line so that the volume fits. If almost everything closes as explained for the same reason, the rule needs an exclusion or a higher minimum denominator. If nothing ever fires, check the feed and the threshold before concluding the unit is clean.

What if our ADC has no override reason field?

Count what you can and record the gap as a program risk. Overrides can still be flagged and counted by user, drug and unit, and each can be matched to an order and pharmacist review after the fact. Without a reason field, the compliance KPI covers only whether an override was reviewed in time, not why it happened. Ask the vendor whether a reason prompt can be turned on, and in the meantime use a short written reason on the pharmacist review step so the rule can learn from the repeating causes.

How do we compare units of different sizes?

Use rates, not counts, and compare each unit with units of the same type and with its own history. Discrepancies per 1,000 doses, overrides per 100 removals and similar ratios remove size, but a small unit still produces unstable rates, so apply a minimum denominator and show the count beside the rate. A rolling 90-day window gives small units enough events. Do not rank an ICU against a med-surg floor; their acuity and cabinet use differ, and the ranking mostly measures that.

When does an indicator become an investigation?

When triage cannot explain it away and at least one escalation condition applies: the pattern recurs after an earlier alert closed, two or more independent indicators point at the same person or drug, or patients may be exposed to risk. A single indicator over its line is a reason for a limited chart review, not an investigation. Write the conversion criteria into policy so that the decision does not depend on who is reviewing that week, and record the reason for each decision.
