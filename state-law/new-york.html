New York drug diversion law

Statutes, board rules, penalties, reporting deadlines and monitoring, as read on 15 November 2026.

New York divides diversion law across three bodies of text: the Education Law, under which the Board of Regents disciplines pharmacists and nurses for professional misconduct; Article 33 of the Public Health Law and its regulations in 10 NYCRR Part 80, administered by the Department of Health's Bureau of Narcotic Enforcement; and the Penal Law, which has an article on criminal diversion of prescription medications. Each statement below links to the text it comes from.

On this page: Statutes · Department of Health rules · Penalties · Reporting duties and deadlines · Monitoring · Sources

Statutes: professional misconduct and criminal diversion

Education Law section 6509 defines professional misconduct for the licensed professions. Among its definitions are practising the profession fraudulently or with gross negligence, practising while the ability to practise is impaired by alcohol, drugs, physical disability or mental disability, and being dependent on, or a habitual user of, narcotics, barbiturates, amphetamines, hallucinogens or other drugs having similar effects [1]. It also covers being convicted of an act constituting a crime under New York, federal or another jurisdiction's law that would be a crime in New York [1].

Article 178 of the Penal Law is headed Criminal diversion of prescription medications and prescriptions. Section 178.00 defines a criminal diversion act as knowingly transferring or delivering a prescription medication or device in exchange for anything of pecuniary value, with knowledge or reasonable grounds to know the recipient has no medical need for it, receiving one from a seller not authorised by law to sell it, or transferring or receiving a prescription in exchange for anything of pecuniary value [2]. Section 178.05 states that the article does not apply to a duly licensed pharmacist acting in good faith in the lawful course of the practice of pharmacy [3].

Separately, Penal Law section 178.26 provides that no person shall willfully obtain or attempt to obtain a controlled substance, a prescription for one or an official New York State prescription form by fraud, deceit, misrepresentation or subterfuge, by concealing a material fact, or by using a false name or address, or make a false statement in a prescription, order, application, report or record required by Public Health Law article 33 [4].

Department of Health rules: Part 80 notification and dispensing reports

The Department of Health's controlled-substance regulations are in Title 10 NYCRR Part 80. Section 80.20, headed Theft or loss, requires the licensee to promptly notify the department of any theft or loss of any controlled substance, on forms the department furnishes, and states that thefts must be reported whether or not the controlled substances are later recovered, the responsible parties identified or action taken against them [5].

Section 80.110, headed Notification by licensee, applies to persons licensed or certified under Public Health Law article 33 and persons authorised to carry out specified activities. It requires prompt notice of each incident or alleged incident of theft, loss or possible diversion of controlled substances manufactured, ordered, distributed or possessed by that person, on a form filed with the Bureau of Narcotic Enforcement [6]. The inclusion of possible diversion matters: the duty is triggered by an alleged incident, not only by a confirmed one.

Section 80.73 sets the dispensing-report timetable for pharmacies. Prescription information must be filed electronically with the Bureau of Narcotic Enforcement not later than 24 hours after the substance was delivered, and a pharmacy that has dispensed no controlled substances for the relevant period must file a zero report [7]. The Commissioner of Health may grant a waiver allowing filings within a longer period on a showing of economic hardship or technological limitation [7].

Penalties: Regents discipline and criminal grades

Education Law section 6511 lists the penalties the Board of Regents may impose on a licensee found guilty of professional misconduct: censure and reprimand; suspension of the licence, wholly for a fixed period, partially until retraining is completed, or wholly until the licensee completes a course of therapy or treatment the Regents prescribe; revocation; annulment of the licence or registration; limitation on registration or issuance of further licences; a fine not exceeding ten thousand dollars on each specification of charges of which the respondent is found guilty; a requirement of education or training; and up to one hundred hours of public service [8].

The Penal Law grades criminal diversion by the value of the benefit exchanged. A criminal diversion act is a class A misdemeanor in the fourth degree; the third degree, a class E felony, applies where the value exceeds one thousand dollars or the person has a prior fourth-degree conviction; the second degree, a class D felony, where the value exceeds three thousand dollars; and the first degree, a class C felony, where it exceeds fifty thousand dollars [9] [10] [11] [12]. Fraud and deceit related to controlled substances under section 178.26 is a class A misdemeanor [4].

Under Penal Law section 70.00, the term for a class C felony is fixed by the court and may not exceed fifteen years, and for a class D felony it may not exceed seven years [13].

Reporting duties and deadlines

New York's reporting language uses the word promptly rather than a fixed number of days for theft, loss and possible diversion: sections 80.20 and 80.110 both require prompt notification to the department on its forms [5] [6]. Because section 80.110 reaches an alleged incident of possible diversion, a pharmacy that has reasonable grounds to suspect an employee should treat the report as due before an investigation concludes.

The fixed deadlines are in the dispensing-data rules. Section 80.73 requires the prescription information to be filed with the Bureau of Narcotic Enforcement not later than 24 hours after delivery, with a longer limit for prescriptions shipped by mail or licensed express delivery service, which must be filed not later than 72 hours after shipment [7]. A zero report is due no later than 14 days following the most recent previously reported dispensing [7].

Public Health Law section 3333 supplies the statutory basis, stating that pharmacies file or cause to be filed prescription information with the department by electronic means on a real time basis as the commissioner requires by regulation, subject to waivers for longer periods where warranted [14].

Monitoring: the PMP registry and professional-assistance routes

Public Health Law section 3343-a establishes the prescription monitoring program registry, an electronic system for collecting, monitoring and reporting information on the prescribing and dispensing of controlled substances. The registry includes information reported by pharmacies on a real time basis, and it must be secure and easily accessible by practitioners and pharmacists [15]. The statute requires every practitioner to consult the registry before prescribing or dispensing any Schedule II, III or IV controlled substance, to review the patient's controlled substance history [15].

The statute also requires the department to monitor and record access to the registry, identifying the authorised individual accessing it and each controlled substance history accessed [15]. Access logs are therefore a record that investigators can use to check whether a person looked up patients they had no reason to.

For licensees whose problem is drug or alcohol abuse, Education Law section 6510-b allows a licensee who may be temporarily incapacitated for active practice, where the problem has not resulted in harm to a patient or client, to voluntarily surrender the licence to the department, which may hold it for the period of incapacity or accept the surrender subject to conditions to be met before restoration [16]. Section 6510-c separately provides for approved nurse peer assistance programmes, whose services include assessing a participant's needs, early identification of drug-related problems and providing information, support and advice [17].
